The ranking function, as six decisions

Between your 5,574 records and the handful that reach the prompt sit six choices. Five were made deliberately and the code argues for each. The sixth was never made at all — and your own sessions show it firing.

Decision 1 — what counts as a term

The match runs against the key mistake only. Not the transcript, not the opponent action, not the positions. Terms are runs of four or more letters, minus a stopword list of the connective words that appear in almost every extracted mistake (practitioner, unable, opponent, while…).

If that leaves zero terms, rankRecords returns an empty array rather than falling back to anything. This is the same instinct as the extraction prompt returning "unknown" rather than guessing a side. Throughout this pipeline, the designed response to "I cannot tell" is to abstain, never to approximate.

Why abstain rather than fall back

With nothing to match against you cannot tell relevance from irrelevance, and injecting arbitrary records for the position is precisely what the blind trial showed makes cues worse. No records is a better outcome than wrong records.

Decision 2 — the gate is a plain count

A record needs GROUNDING_MIN_RELEVANCE = 2 distinct matched terms. Not a weighted score, not a similarity — a count.

The value was bought with evidence. Injecting the top 20 records for a position made cues worse: a practitioner reading blind preferred the ungrounded cue two-to-one. The records were about the right position and the wrong problem, and the model dutifully built a confident, specific cue around a mechanic that answered a different question. Measured against the frozen baseline, a bar of 2 keeps 17 of 23 resolvable sessions grounded while cutting the median injected set from 25 to 4.

You can see the gate biting in your own data. One session had 137 candidate records for its positions and exactly one cleared the bar. That is the gate working, not failing.

Decision 3 — but the order is weighted

This is the distinction that repays understanding, and it is easy to read past: the gate is a count, the ordering is weighted. The same matched terms are used twice, scored two different ways.

Ordering multiplies each matched term's inverse document frequency by 2 when the term is in the sport's own vocabulary. That second factor exists because of one specific failure:

The case that forced it

For the mistake "failed to secure the Kimura, allowing the opponent to escape to Turtle", the words allowing and kimura each appeared in exactly 13 of 295 candidate records. Identical IDF. So allowing+turtle tied kimura+turtle, nine records scored exactly 2, and file order picked the winner — a record about hip connection outranked the one naming the actual technique.

Rarity measures how unusual a word is. It does not measure how much the word identifies a technique. allowing is rare because it is connective tissue from the mistake sentence; kimura is rare because few techniques are the Kimura. Only the sport vocabulary separates them.

Note the direction of the problem: a bigger corpus makes this worse, not better. More records clear a low bar, so more of them pile into the tie, and the tiebreak was never meaningful. Yours has grown to 5,574.

Decision 4 — human review outranks keyword overlap

Decision 5 — the cap is about dilution, not cost

Twenty records is roughly 2,100 tokens on a prompt that is otherwise tiny, so money was never the constraint. The constraint is that burying the one relevant correction among 145 others makes the model choose badly.

In your data the cap is binding on six of eight grounded sessions — they injected exactly 20 because 20 was the ceiling, not because 20 was all that qualified. The other two injected 1 apiece. There is no middle.

Decision 6 — the one nobody made

Filed as srjt/flowlog#114 after this lesson was written.

Before any of the above runs, the records have to be fetched:

coaching_records?select=*&sport_key=eq.bjj&position=in.(…)&limit=200

That limit=200 has no order by beside it. When a session's positions hold more than 200 records between them, Postgres returns some 200 of them and ranking never sees the rest.

5,574records total
9positions over 200 alone
1,114records in standing
3of your sessions hit exactly 200

The consequence is worse than "some records were missed", and it is worth being precise about why:

  • The pool is arbitrary. Without order by, which 200 rows come back is not specified by SQL. It is whatever the plan produces.
  • The weights are computed over that arbitrary pool. IDF in rankRecords is measured against the candidate set, deliberately — "the pool is already narrowed to a position and that is the set the choice is actually made within". When the pool is a truncated slice of the real one, the rarity of every term is measured against the wrong denominator. The ordering shifts, not just the membership.
  • It is silent. grounding_candidates records 200 and looks like a healthy pool. Nothing distinguishes "200 records existed" from "at least 200 existed and we took some".

Is it at least the same 200 every time? Yes — today

Six consecutive runs of the identical query returned the same 200 ids in the same order. So there is no determinism bug to report, and re-analysis is not currently re-grounding sessions differently.

But that stability is not promised by anything. A query with no order by may return rows in any order, and this one is stable only because the plan and the heap happen to be stable. A vacuum, an update to a record, a new publish, or a plan change can reshuffle it — and the day it does, ADR 0011's promise that re-analysis must not change which records ground the cue breaks silently. Stable by accident is not the same as stable.

One concrete skew worth seeing

For standing, records published in the most recent batch are reachable at 7 of 422 — 1.7%, against 27.3% for the batch before it. That is a real instance, but it is not a general law: for half-guard-bottom the newest batches are tiny and fully reachable. The bias depends on where rows sit in the heap, not on when they were published. The defensible claim is the blind rate above; the publish-recency story is an artefact you should not build on.

Check yourself

A session's key mistake yields zero usable terms. What does rankRecords return?

An empty array. With no terms you cannot tell relevance from irrelevance, and injecting arbitrary records for the position is exactly what the blind trial showed makes cues worse. Abstaining is the designed outcome, not a degraded one.

Two records both match exactly two terms. What decides which ranks higher?

The gate is a count; the order is weighted. Both clear the gate at 2, then IDF over the candidate pool orders them, with a domain term counting double. Input order is only the last tiebreak — and the Kimura case is what happens when it becomes the first one that discriminates.

Why is certified a tiebreak rather than a requirement?

It would have grounded nothing. 0 of 1,322 records were certified. Making it a gate switches grounding off entirely until the review queue is finished; making it a tiebreak lets review improve ranking smoothly from the first vote. Note that rejected and contested are gates — the asymmetry is deliberate.

Why does a growing corpus make the ranking problem worse rather than better?

More records clear the bar. The gate is a count of two, so a bigger store means more records scoring exactly 2, more of them tied, and the tiebreak deciding more often. That is why the Kimura collision surfaced when the corpus grew rather than at the start.

Your session reports grounding_candidates = 200. What do you now know?

At least 200, and the pool was cut. 200 is the query's own limit, so the number is a ceiling report, not a count. Worse, the IDF weights were computed over that slice — so truncation changed the ordering of what survived, not merely what was available.

The habit to take from this

Every constant in grounding.ts has a story attached in a comment, and every story is a measurement. Before you change one, find its story — the value you are about to replace was almost certainly the second thing tried.
